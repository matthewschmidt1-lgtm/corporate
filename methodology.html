Method

How scoring works.

A conversation tool, not a validated assessment. It shows how you see your company. Here is every step, so you can judge it.

What it asks

Each card describes a realistic situation and asks what actually happens, not what should. There are four kinds of answer: Entrepreneurial, Well run, Bureaucratic, and Self-protective. You can also answer "it depends" or "I have not seen this." Cards test eleven principles drawn from the lessons of Steve Jobs's career: focus, simplicity, customer obsession, excellence, innovation, talent, ownership, speed, integration, courage and human leadership. The questions are about behavior, not about whether you like your employer.

How each answer is scored

An answer scores by how well it fits the stakes of the situation. The card sets the stakes, not you. Controls where money, safety, privacy or the law are involved earn full credit. Speed without controls there does not.

"It depends" scores 50. "I have not seen this" is left out. Your industry setting nudges medium-stakes cards slightly. In heavily regulated industries, four named cards (for example trialing a tool with customer data, or wording that needs legal review) are treated as higher stakes, so legitimate process there is not counted as bureaucracy. The report shows what the score would be without the industry setting. These numbers are judgment, not measurements.

How the overall score is built

Each of the eleven principles counts equally. A principle's score is the average of the cards that test it. Cards you may not be able to see from where you sit count 0.7 instead of 1. The overall score is the average across principles. A principle shows a number only with 4 or more cards behind it, a band with 2 or 3, and nothing with fewer.

Two editions, two decks

The Employee edition draws from cards people at any level can see, plus everyday ones such as expense approvals, access requests and how news reaches staff. The Chief Officer edition draws from cards leaders can see, such as how exceptions are logged, what reaches the top, board materials and audit findings. Each edition covers all eleven principles.

How the culture type is chosen

The type comes from the pattern of your answers, not from the score alone. Two things are measured separately: slow process (approvals, hierarchy) and self-protection (self-protection, blame, politics). A company can have either, both or neither. If many answers were "it depends," or answers within a principle pull far apart, the type is "Two Companies in One Building." Cruelty or pressure that results are allowed to excuse (two or more such answers on human leadership) caps the result below the top types. In heavily regulated industries the friction thresholds are looser, because a well-run regulated company legitimately carries more structure.

Quality flags lower the level by one: nearly all answers the same, many "have not seen," answers that contradict each other on the same behavior, answering very fast, or tapping the same screen position. The score range shows how much the cards you happened to see could move the result. It does not show whether the method is right.

What the results can tell you

  • How one person perceives recurring patterns at their workplace.
  • Whether the problems look more like slow structure or self-protection.
  • Relative strengths and weaknesses across the eleven principles for the same person.

What it cannot tell you

  • The company's real performance, or whether it will succeed.
  • Whether one company is better than another. There are no norms, and no comparison data.
  • Anything about an individual. It is not an HR, hiring, performance or legal tool.
  • That the eleven principles cause good results. They are one interpretation of a biography.
  • A precise number. At typical lengths the real range is 10 to 35 points wide.

Known limits

The weights, cutoffs and principle list are the author's judgment. The game has not been tested on a large group of real respondents, so nothing here is a statistical finding. A game with fewer cards can land in a different type on a replay. Answers can be influenced by wanting to look good, or by one bad week. Treat the result as a prompt for a conversation, and test it against what you know.
