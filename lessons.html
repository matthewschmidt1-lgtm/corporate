The lessons

Results slipped.
The meeting count did not.

Bureaucracy is what happens when a company forgets who it works for. This is the field guide behind the game: five questions, ten rules, and what to do about it. Read it before the next steering committee. Or instead of it.

Part one

Five questions that will change how you think about business.

Memorize these. They are worth more than every date, name and anecdote from the life of Steve Jobs, and nobody will quiz you on the dates.

1Focus

If I could only work on one thing for the next 12 months, what would create the biggest result?

In 1997 Apple had too many products, too many priorities and no direction. Jobs came back and collapsed the lineup into four boxes: consumer desktop, consumer portable, professional desktop, professional portable. That was the strategy. It fit on a napkin, and it saved the company.

What bureaucracy says: "We cannot pick. Everything is tier one."

Monday. Write down the three things eating most of your time. Circle the one that produces results. Stop the other two. Your ability to say no sets the quality of your yes.

2Value

What problem do people have that I can solve 10 times better?

Apple did not ask how to build a better phone. It asked why anyone needs a physical keyboard, and how the internet could feel natural in your hand. The iPhone changed the experience. The spec sheet was an afterthought.

What bureaucracy says: "Ten percent better, with a roadmap and a launch event."

Monday. Stop asking "what should we sell." Ask "what frustrating problem can we make disappear." People do not buy your process. They buy the relief.

3Simplicity

What am I doing that I could eliminate?

"1,000 songs in your pocket." Digital music was a mess of files, formats and cables. The iPod made it one sentence. Easy things are sometimes simple. Complex things made simple take extraordinary work, which is why most companies skip it.

What bureaucracy says: "Let's add a step so we can track it."

Monday. If buying takes ten steps, make it three. If the product has twenty features, find the five that carry the value. If the pitch takes five minutes, get it down to fifteen seconds.

4Excellence

What would I improve if my reputation depended on every single detail?

Jobs insisted the insides of the Macintosh be designed with care, though almost no customer would ever see them. Excellence that only shows up when someone is watching is just theater.

What bureaucracy says: "That's within tolerance."

Monday. Audit what customers actually touch. How fast do you reply? How clean is the presentation? How easy is it to buy? What happens after the sale? Your reputation does depend on every detail. Nobody has been telling you.

5Innovation

What does everyone in my industry accept as normal that I could challenge?

Jobs did not try to beat competitors on price or specs. He tried to change what customers expected, until the alternatives stopped being considered. Some limits are real. Many are just old habits with good posture.

What bureaucracy says: "That's how it's done here."

Monday. Write down three things your industry treats as law. For each, ask one question: real limitation, or outdated assumption?

Why this works

He was not the best engineer, the best businessman, or the nicest leader.

He won by combining five unusual abilities. Most executives have two. Almost none have the third one, and it is the one that matters.

  1. He knew what to focus on.
  2. He held extraordinary standards for quality.
  3. He understood how technology meets human experience.
  4. He made products people did not yet know they wanted.
  5. He built teams that could turn ambitious ideas into real things.

Field notes

Six things that are harder than they sound.

Start with the experience, not the technology.

A restaurant sells an evening. A gym sells a different person. A software company sells your afternoon back. If your roadmap is organized by technology, you are describing your plumbing to people who came for a drink.

Study something that is not your industry.

Typography on the Mac. Psychology in the iPod. Engineers ask whether it can be built. Designers ask whether it should work that way. Customers ask why they should care. You need all three in the room, and the room is usually all engineers.

Build an A-team, not a big team.

A small group of exceptional people beats a large group of average ones. Hire for ability and character. Give them ownership and a mission. Then remove whatever stands between them and the work. That obstacle is frequently a process you wrote.

Separate impossible from unprecedented.

Impossible is a law of physics. Difficult is a schedule. Unprecedented is a thing nobody tried. "We have always done it differently" is none of those. Ask the team which one they mean.

Own the whole customer journey.

The iPod worked because iTunes worked. Hardware, software and content were designed together. You do not need to own your supply chain. You do need to answer for the experience, including shipping, support and returns.

Treat a setback as raw material.

In 1985 Jobs was pushed out of the company he started. He built NeXT, which struggled, then put money into Pixar, which did not. Apple later bought NeXT, and its technology became the foundation of the Mac and iPhone operating systems. Do not let your identity depend on one logo.

The field guide

A process should serve the work. Not the other way around.

A healthy organization uses processes to help people do great work. An unhealthy bureaucracy makes people do work to maintain the processes.

Apple became a large corporation with plenty of process of its own. Jobs's real job was keeping those systems from overwhelming creativity and execution. Nobody escapes bureaucracy. You just stop feeding it.

    What not to copy.

    Many people read about Jobs and conclude that arrogance is the secret. It is not. Every strength he had had a bill attached.

    The strengthWhen it goes bad
    PerfectionismBecomes obsession.
    ConfidenceBecomes arrogance.
    High standardsBecome unreasonable demands.
    Strong opinionsBecome dismissiveness.
    FocusBecomes neglect.
    PersuasionBecomes manipulation.
    Emotional intensityDamages relationships.

    Copy the standards. Skip the cruelty.

    His delay in treating pancreatic cancer is the sober footnote: conviction that overrides evidence is not strength, in business or anywhere else. The goal is an exceptional leader, not a difficult person.

    The final lesson

    Think differently.

    Dropped out of college Explored spirituality Studied calligraphy Helped build Apple Got fired Built NeXT Transformed Pixar Returned to Apple

    None of it looked connected at the time. Later it shaped how he approached design, technology and business. The lesson is not the route. The lesson is that curiosity compounds.

    • Learn outside your profession.
    • Explore different industries.
    • Pay attention to what people love.
    • Develop your taste.
    • Connect ideas other people do not connect.

    Do not spend your life trying to become the next Steve Jobs.
    Become someone who sees what everyone else walked past.
