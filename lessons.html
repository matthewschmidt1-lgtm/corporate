The lessons

Results slipped.
The meeting count did not.

Bureaucracy is what happens when a company forgets who it works for, and why it was built. This is the field guide behind the game: five questions and what to do about it. Read it instead of the next steering committee.

Start here

Five questions in the spirit of Jobs.

Read these once. Use them on Monday.

1Focus

If I could only work on one thing for the next 12 months, what would create the biggest result?

In 1997 Apple had too many products, too many priorities and no direction. Jobs came back and collapsed the lineup into four boxes: consumer desktop, consumer portable, professional desktop, professional portable. That was the strategy. It fit on a whiteboard, and it was the core of the turnaround.

What bureaucracy says: "We cannot pick. Everything is tier one."

Monday. Write down the three things eating most of your time. Circle the one that produces results. Stop the other two. Your ability to say no sets the quality of your yes.

2Customer

What problem do people have that I can solve 10 times better?

Jobs started with the experience a person should have, then worked back to the technology. Phones were clumsy to use, so the iPhone was built to fix that. The experience came first.

What bureaucracy says: "Ten percent better, with a roadmap and a launch event."

Monday. Stop asking "what should we sell." Ask "what frustrating problem can we make disappear." People do not buy your process. They buy the relief.

3Simplicity

What am I doing that I could eliminate?

"1,000 songs in your pocket." Digital music was a mess of files, formats and cables. The iPod made it one sentence. Easy things are often simple. Complex things made simple take extraordinary work, which is why most companies skip it.

What bureaucracy says: "Let's add a step so we can track it."

Monday. If buying takes ten steps, make it three. If the product has twenty features, find the five that carry the value. If the pitch needs a deck, get it down to one sentence.

4Excellence

What would I improve if my reputation depended on every single detail?

His father taught him to finish the back of a cabinet, even though nobody would ever see it. Jobs insisted the insides of the Macintosh be designed with care for the same reason. Excellence that only shows up when someone is watching is just theater.

What bureaucracy says: "That's within tolerance."

Monday. Audit what customers actually touch. How fast do you reply? How clean is the presentation? How easy is it to buy? What happens after the sale? Your reputation does depend on every detail. Nobody has been telling you.

5Innovation

What does everyone in my industry accept as normal that I could challenge?

Jobs aimed less at beating rivals on specs than at changing what customers expected. Some limits are real. Many are just old habits with good posture.

What bureaucracy says: "That's how it's done here."

Monday. Write down three things your industry treats as law. For each, ask one question: real limitation, or outdated assumption?

Field notes

Six things that are harder than they sound.

Start with the experience, not the technology.

A restaurant sells an evening. A gym sells a different person. A software company sells your afternoon back. If your roadmap is organized by technology, you are describing your plumbing to people who came for a drink.

Study something that is not your industry.

A calligraphy class he dropped in on shaped the typography of the Mac. Engineers ask whether it can be built. Designers ask whether it should work that way. Customers ask why they should care. You need all three in the room, and the room is usually all engineers.

Build an A-team, not a big team.

A small group of exceptional people beats a large group of average ones. Hire for ability and character. Give them ownership and a mission. Then remove whatever stands between them and the work. That obstacle is frequently a process you wrote.

Separate impossible from unprecedented.

Impossible is a law of physics. Difficult is a schedule. Unprecedented is a thing nobody tried. "We have always done it differently" is none of those. Ask the team which one they mean.

Own the whole customer journey.

The iPod worked because iTunes worked. Hardware, software and content were designed together. You do not need to own your supply chain. You do need to answer for the experience, including shipping, support and returns.

Treat a setback as raw material.

In 1985 Jobs was pushed out of the company he started. He built NeXT, which struggled, and bought Pixar, which took years to pay off. Apple later bought NeXT, and its technology became the foundation of the Mac and iPhone operating systems. Do not let your identity depend on one logo.

The field guide

A process should serve the work. Not the other way around.

A healthy organization uses processes to help people do great work. An unhealthy bureaucracy makes people do work to maintain the processes.

Apple had plenty of process. Jobs kept it from running the place. Nobody escapes bureaucracy. You just stop feeding it.

The deeper problem

The day a company loses its soul.

Bureaucracy is the symptom. The cause is usually quieter: the company stopped doing what it was built to do. As companies grow, culture drifts, values turn into slogans, and words begin to replace actions.

  1. 01The mission becomes a slogan.People talk about the vision but stop living it.
  2. 02Politics replace progress.Protecting positions becomes more important than solving problems.
  3. 03Transparency becomes a buzzword.Leaders talk about openness but withhold information and avoid difficult conversations.
  4. 04Values become window dressing.What the company says it believes no longer matches how people are treated or how decisions are made.
  5. 05Excellence becomes negotiable.Good enough replaces great, and accountability slowly disappears.

A company's culture is not what it says on the walls. It is what people experience every day.

Never become so busy running the company that you forget why you built it.

Monday. Ask five people, separately, what the company is for. If you get five different answers, you have found the real problem. If the answers are all slogans, you have found the second one.

The other side

Ten rules for a company that refuses to calcify.

    Test your own company

    The honest version

    Jobs got it wrong too.

    A field guide that only tells the wins is a brochure. Here are three of his misses, and what each one teaches.

    The first Macintosh was beautiful and not enough.

    The 1984 Macintosh launched to huge attention, but it had little memory, was slow, and cost more than many buyers could justify. Sales fell after the first rush. Perfection that ignores the customer's budget and needs is a hobby.

    Conviction cost him his company.

    In 1985 the board and the chief executive he had recruited sided against him. His brilliance was real, and so was the damage his harshness did to the people around him. Standards without respect do not scale.

    NeXT loved the product more than the market.

    The NeXT computer was elegant and expensive, aimed at buyers who could not afford it, and it sold in small numbers. The company survived by pivoting to software. Loving what you make is not a business model.

    The lesson that ties them together.

    He improved because he was forced to. The Jobs who returned to Apple in 1997 kept the taste and added discipline. The skill is not being right the first time. It is learning faster than the mistake can hurt you.

    Read this part twice

    What not to copy.

    Many people read about Jobs and conclude that arrogance is the secret. It is not. Every strength he had had a bill attached.

    The strengthWhen it goes bad
    PerfectionismBecomes obsession.
    ConfidenceBecomes arrogance.
    High standardsBecome unreasonable demands.
    Strong opinionsBecome dismissiveness.
    PersuasionBecomes manipulation.
    Emotional intensityDamages relationships.

    Copy the standards. Skip the cruelty.

    He put off cancer surgery for about nine months. Conviction that overrides evidence is not strength, in business or anywhere else. The goal is an exceptional leader, not a difficult person.

    The final lesson

    Think differently.

    None of it looked connected at the time. A calligraphy class he dropped in on later shaped the typography of the Mac. The lesson is not the route. The lesson is that curiosity compounds.

    Do not spend your life trying to become the next Steve Jobs.
    Become someone who sees what everyone else walked past.

    Your challenge

    This week, write down in one sentence why this company exists. Then find one thing you did this month that contradicts it. Fix that one thing, and tell someone you did.
