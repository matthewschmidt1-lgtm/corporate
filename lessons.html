Inspired by the thinking of Steve Jobs, five principles for leaders who want to challenge the status quo and get their organizations back on track.

The Corporate Speed Trap

When success starts slowing you down.

Great companies don't lose their entrepreneurial spirit overnight. They lose it gradually through unnecessary layers, competing priorities, internal politics, fear of speaking up, and the growing gap between what leadership says and what people actually experience.

The values are on the walls.
The reality is in the hallways.

Seven questions every CEO should ask

  1. Customer proximity

    Are we making decisions too far removed from the customer?

    Do we understand what customers actually experience, or are we relying on filtered information, internal assumptions, or secondhand opinions?

  2. Organizational complexity

    Have we created too many layers between ideas and action?

    How much time and energy are spent navigating internal processes instead of solving problems, making decisions, and moving the business forward?

  3. Empowerment

    Are our best people trusted and empowered to make decisions?

    Or do we preach ownership while keeping authority at the top, forcing people to seek permission instead of taking action?

  4. Accountability

    Are we measuring activity or real results?

    Do people take ownership of outcomes, or simply focus on completing assigned tasks and ticking boxes?

  5. The courage to reinvent

    Are we protecting yesterday's success at the expense of tomorrow's opportunities?

    Are we willing to challenge the business models that made us successful, even when they're still profitable?

  6. Values versus reality

    Do our actions reflect the values we claim to stand for?

    Can people challenge leadership without fear of repercussions? Can they speak honestly when the truth is uncomfortable? Are people treated fairly, regardless of their position or title?

  7. Transparency versus politics

    Does information flow openly, or is it filtered, controlled and withheld?

    Do people understand why decisions are made? Are mistakes and failures discussed honestly? Can employees raise uncomfortable questions without being labelled difficult or disloyal?

The values reality test

Don't ask whether you value trust. Look at what happens.

  4. We believe in accountabilityResponsibility shifts when things go wrong

The consequence

People stop telling the truth.

They tell leadership what it wants to hear. Problems stay hidden. Decisions slow down. Trust disappears. And the CEO may believe the culture is healthy, because every report has been filtered through several layers on its way up.

A company cannot move quickly when people spend more energy managing perceptions than solving problems.

The uncomfortable question

What happens when telling the truth becomes harder than telling people what they want to hear?

A company's true values aren't revealed by what it puts on its walls. They're revealed by what happens when someone challenges the people in power.

Inspired by 27 years of Steve Jobs in his own words.

  1. 1. Focus

    If we could only get one thing right over the next 12 months, what would make the biggest difference?

    Monday. Write down the three things consuming most of your time. Which one will genuinely move the business forward? Protect it. Delegate, defer, or stop the other two.

    Focus isn't about doing more with less time. It's about having the courage to stop doing what doesn't matter. The power of your yes comes from the strength of your no.

    The story

    In 1997 Apple had too many products, too many priorities and no direction. Jobs came back and collapsed the lineup into four boxes: consumer desktop, consumer portable, professional desktop, professional portable. That was the strategy. It fit on a whiteboard, and it was the core of the turnaround.

    What bureaucracy says: "We cannot pick. Everything is tier one."

  2. 2. Customer

    What do our customers need, want, or value that we could deliver better than anyone else?

    Monday. Stop asking, "What should we sell?" Start asking, "What matters to our customers, and how can we deliver it better?"

    Customers don't buy your products. They buy what those products bring to their lives. Understand that, and the product becomes the answer.

    The story

    Jobs started with the experience a person should have, then worked back to the technology. Phones were clumsy to use, so the iPhone was built to fix that. The experience came first.

    What bureaucracy says: "Ten percent better, with a roadmap and a launch event."

  3. 3. Simplicity

    What am I doing that I could eliminate?

    Monday. If buying takes ten steps, make it three. If the product has twenty features, find the five that carry the value. If the pitch needs a deck, get it down to one sentence.

    The story

    "1,000 songs in your pocket." Digital music was a mess of files, formats and cables. The iPod made it one sentence. Easy things are often simple. Complex things made simple take extraordinary work, which is why most companies skip it.

    What bureaucracy says: "Let's add a step so we can track it."

  4. 4. Excellence

    What would we improve if being great mattered more than being good enough?

    Monday. Look at every part of the customer experience. How quickly do you respond? How good is the quality? How easy is it to buy? What happens after the sale?

    Good enough is easy. Excellence takes intention, discipline, and attention to detail.

    The story

    His father taught him to finish the back of a cabinet, even though nobody would ever see it. Jobs insisted the insides of the Macintosh be designed with care for the same reason. Excellence that only shows up when someone is watching is just theater.

    What bureaucracy says: "That's within tolerance."

  5. 5. Innovation

    What does everyone in my industry accept as normal that I could challenge?

    Monday. Write down three things your industry treats as law. For each, ask one question: real limitation, or outdated assumption?

    The story

    Jobs aimed less at beating rivals on specs than at changing what customers expected. Some limits are real. Many are just old habits with good posture.

    What bureaucracy says: "That's how it's done here."

Now let's look at what happens when you put these principles into practice.

Field notes

Four lessons that are harder than they sound.

Start with the experience, not the product.

Customers don't just buy products or services. They buy the value, enjoyment, and experiences they deliver. Design the entire experience around what customers value, from the first interaction to what happens after the sale. Every touchpoint matters.

Customer thinkingStart with the experience.

Study something that is not your industry.

A calligraphy class he dropped in on shaped the typography of the Mac. Engineers ask whether it can be built. Designers ask whether it should work that way. Customers ask why they should care. You need all three in the room, and the room is usually all engineers.

Creative thinkingLook beyond your industry.

Build an A-team, not a big team.

A small group of exceptional people beats a large group of average ones. Hire for ability and character. Give them ownership and a mission. Then remove whatever stands between them and the work. That obstacle is frequently a process you wrote.

Jobs found that A players police themselves. Gather enough of them and they only want to work with, and hire, more A players. Let B and C players in, and the bar drops for everyone.

38:52 Steve Jobs, the Lost Interview, 1995

PeopleBuild exceptional teams.

Separate impossible from unprecedented.

Impossible is a law of physics. Difficult is a matter of effort. Unprecedented simply means nobody has done it before.

The greatest breakthroughs often begin where conventional thinking ends. Challenge assumptions, question limitations, and don't mistake "we've never done it" for "it can't be done." Even setbacks can become the starting point for something extraordinary. After leaving Apple in 1985, Jobs built NeXT, whose technology would eventually help shape the Mac and iPhone.

MindsetChallenge the accepted. Rethink the impossible.

Individual thinking changes how you work. Organizational thinking changes how the entire company works.

The field guide

Processes should accelerate performance, not obstruct it.

The best organizations use structure to empower people, not burden them. When process takes priority over progress, people spend more time maintaining the system than getting the work done.

Apple had plenty of process. Jobs kept it from running the place. Nobody escapes bureaucracy. You just stop feeding it.

The iceberg of bureaucracy.

Bureaucracy is the symptom. Something deeper is broken.

The meetings, approvals, delays, and excuses are what you see. Beneath them are six deeper failures that slowly undermine an organization's ability to perform.

  1. 1

    The mission becomes a slogan.

    What happens
    The company talks about its mission but stops using it to guide decisions.
    What to change
    Make the mission part of everyday priorities, resource allocation, and customer decisions.
  2. 2

    Execution becomes the excuse.

    What happens
    When results disappoint, leadership demands more focus and better execution. But nobody questions whether the strategy is right, priorities are clear, or people know what they're accountable for.
    What to change
    Hold leadership accountable for the strategy, not just execution. Align priorities, resources, ownership, and measurable results.
  3. 3

    Politics replace progress.

    What happens
    Protecting status and departmental interests becomes more important than solving problems.
    What to change
    Reward results, collaboration, and the best ideas, regardless of rank.
  4. 4

    Transparency becomes a buzzword.

    What happens
    Leaders talk about openness but withhold information and avoid difficult conversations.
    What to change
    Share information, communicate honestly, and make it safe to raise problems.
  5. 5

    Values become window dressing.

    What happens
    What the company rewards and tolerates contradicts what it claims to believe.
    What to change
    Make values visible in hiring, promotions, performance reviews, and leadership behavior.
  6. 6

    Excellence becomes negotiable.

    What happens
    Good enough replaces great. Standards slip, problems go unresolved, and accountability fades.
    What to change
    Set clear standards, empower talented people, and hold everyone accountable for quality and follow through.

Culture isn't what a company says it values. It's what its everyday decisions, feedback, incentives, and leadership behaviors consistently reinforce.

Monday. Ask five people, separately, what the company is for. If you get five different answers, you have found the real problem. If the answers are all slogans, you have found the second one.

Knowing what's broken is useful. Knowing what to build instead is better.

    The honest version

    Jobs got it wrong too.

    A field guide that only celebrates the wins is a brochure. Here's the other side: three significant failures and six strengths that, taken too far, became weaknesses. The lesson isn't that arrogance makes great leaders. It's that brilliance without humility, discipline, and respect can come at a steep price.

    The missesThree times he got it wrong, and what each one teaches
    1. The first Macintosh was beautiful, but not enough.The 1984 Macintosh captured the world's attention, but limited memory, slow performance, and a high price made it difficult for many customers to justify. The initial excitement faded as its limitations became clear.It doesn't matter how brilliant the product is if customers don't see the value.
    2. Conviction cost him his company.In 1985, Jobs lost a power struggle with the board and John Sculley, the CEO he had recruited. His vision was extraordinary, but his confrontational leadership style had damaged relationships and made collaboration increasingly difficult.Strong convictions don't excuse treating people poorly.
    3. NeXT loved the product more than the market.The NeXT computer was beautifully designed but expensive and aimed at a market too small to sustain the business. The company eventually shifted its focus to software.Loving what you make doesn't mean the market will love it too.

    The bill

    Six strengths, and what happens when they go too far.

    The strengthThe downside
    1. PerfectionismObsession
    2. ConfidenceArrogance
    3. High standardsUnreasonable demands
    4. Strong opinionsDismissiveness
    5. PersuasionManipulation
    6. Emotional intensityDamaged relationships

    The real lesson

    Jobs didn't become a great leader simply by being right. He had to learn that vision needs discipline, conviction needs humility, and high standards need respect.

    The Jobs who returned to Apple in 1997 brought the same ambition and product instincts, but a more disciplined approach to building products and organizations.

    Learn from the brilliance. Build on the lessons.

    The final principle

    Think differently.

    At the time, none of it seemed connected. A calligraphy class Steve Jobs casually took would later influence the typography of the Macintosh.

    That's the power of curiosity. You never know where what you learn today will lead, or when it might become tomorrow's breakthrough.
